Purpose of This Guide 
The National Innovation Convening brings together leaders and stakeholders from across the 
U.S. innovation ecosystem to explore how the nation can more effectively move promising 
discoveries from research to translation, commercialization, adoption, and societal impact. 
This resource guide provides a common intellectual and policy foundation to inform the 
discussions. It combines: 
Part I — Evidence Base: Selected Peer-Reviewed Research 
This section highlights research addressing innovation, artificial intelligence, commercialization, 
technology transfer, innovation ecosystems, and the barriers that often separate discovery from 
impact. 
Part II — National Innovation Policy & Strategy Resources 
Selected federal and National Academies resources illustrating current strategies, programs, and 
policy approaches designed to strengthen U.S. innovation capacity. 
Part III — Pre-Convening Reflection Questions 
This section provides questions designed to help participants arrive prepared to contribute ideas 
and actionable recommendations to the National Innovation Roadmap. 
These resources are recommended rather than required reading. Participants are encouraged 
to review the materials most relevant to your expertise and experience.

PART I 
EVIDENCE BASE — SELECTED PEER-
REVIEWED RESEARCH 
1. Building the Capacity to Innovate 
Cohen, W. M., & Levinthal, D. A. (1990). Absorptive capacity: A new perspective on 
learning and innovation. Administrative Science Quarterly, 35(1), 128–152. 
Why it matters 
This landmark article introduced the concept of absorptive capacity—the ability of an 
organization to recognize valuable external knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it commercially. 
The authors argue that prior knowledge and organizational learning capacity strongly influence 
innovation performance. 
For the NIC, the concept raises an important question: producing excellent research is not 
enough. Organizations, regions, and innovation ecosystems must also possess the ability to 
identify, absorb, translate, and deploy knowledge. 
Consider: 
What capabilities do universities, companies, federal agencies, and regions need in order to 
recognize and rapidly act on promising discoveries? 
DOI: 10.2307/2393553 
2. Open Innovation and Cross-Sector Collaboration 
Bogers, M., Chesbrough, H., & Moedas, C. (2018). Open innovation: Research, practices, 
and policies. California Management Review, 60(2), 5–16. 
Why it matters 
Innovation increasingly occurs across organizational boundaries rather than entirely within 
individual institutions. This article examines open innovation at the intersection of research, 
organizational practice, and public policy and discusses opportunities and challenges created by 
external knowledge flows and collaboration. 
This framework is highly relevant to the NIC, where participants represent universities, 
companies, investors, entrepreneurs, government, technology-transfer organizations, and other 
parts of the innovation system.

Consider: 
What institutional or policy barriers make it unnecessarily difficult for knowledge, technologies, 
talent, and capital to move across sectors? 
DOI: 10.1177/0008125617745086 
3. From Discovery to Transformative Innovation 
Pettigrew, R. I., & Cooke, J. P. (2022). At the nexus of science, engineering, and medicine: 
Pasteur's quadrant reconsidered. PNAS Nexus, 1(3), pgac092. 
Why it matters 
The authors reconsider the traditional distinction between basic and applied research and 
emphasize the importance of convergence, interdisciplinary exchange, and movement among 
research approaches in producing transformative innovation. 
The article encourages a more dynamic understanding of the pathway from discovery to impact. 
Consider: 
Should federal research systems more intentionally support movement between basic research, 
use-inspired research, application, prototyping, and commercialization? 
DOI: 10.1093/pnasnexus/pgac092 
Artificial Intelligence and Innovation 
4. Generative AI and Professional Productivity 
Noy, S., & Zhang, W. (2023). Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of 
generative artificial intelligence. Science, 381(6654), 187–192. 
Why it matters 
In a preregistered experiment involving 453 college-educated professionals, researchers found 
that access to generative AI significantly increased productivity: average completion time 
declined by approximately 40%, while output quality increased by approximately 18%. 
The findings suggest that AI can significantly alter how knowledge work is performed. 
Consider: 
If similar productivity improvements can occur in research, engineering, entrepreneurship, 
technology transfer, and commercialization, where could AI most significantly shorten the 
innovation cycle?

5. Generative AI, Expertise, and Knowledge Diffusion 
Brynjolfsson, E., Li, D., & Raymond, L. (2025). Generative AI at work. The Quarterly 
Journal of Economics, 140(2), 889–942. 
Why it matters 
This large-scale study examined the introduction of a generative AI assistant among 5,172 
customer-support agents. The research found significant productivity benefits and provides 
important evidence regarding how AI can transfer knowledge and capability across a workforce. 
For innovation systems, this raises the possibility that AI could help broaden access to 
specialized knowledge that historically resided within a relatively small number of experts. 
Consider: 
Could AI democratize access to expertise in areas such as entrepreneurship, intellectual property, 
regulatory navigation, engineering design, market assessment, and commercialization? 
DOI: 10.1093/qje/qjae044 
6. Understanding the Limits of AI 
Dell'Acqua, F., et al. (2026). Navigating the jagged technological frontier: Field 
experimental evidence of the effects of artificial intelligence on knowledge worker 
productivity and quality. Organization Science, 37(2), 403–423. 
Why it matters 
A preregistered experiment involving 758 knowledge workers demonstrated that AI significantly 
improved performance on tasks falling within its capabilities, but the researchers also identified 
an important “jagged technological frontier”: AI is highly capable on some tasks while 
remaining unreliable on seemingly similar ones. 
This finding is important for avoiding an overly simplistic assumption that AI automatically 
improves every aspect of innovation. 
Consider: 
Where should innovators rely heavily on AI, and where must human expertise, judgment, 
validation, and oversight remain dominant? 
DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2025.21838

Commercialization and the “Valley of Death” 
7. Open Innovation and the Commercialization Gap 
Stefan, I. (2022). Does open innovation enable or hinder crossing the valley of death? 
International Journal of Innovation Management, 26(9). 
Why it matters 
The article examines whether open innovation can help organizations move technologies from 
R&D to commercialization. It finds that collaboration can help bridge the commercialization gap 
through mechanisms such as collaborative platforms and inter-industry partnerships, but 
collaboration can also introduce challenges including conflicting interests and opportunistic 
behavior. 
Consider: 
What forms of collaboration are most effective at moving technologies across the valley of 
death—and what makes some partnerships fail? 
DOI: 10.1142/S1363919622400254 
8. Understanding the Challenges Facing Technology 
Startups 
Gbadegeshin, S. A., et al. (2022). Overcoming the valley of death: A new model for high 
technology startups. Sustainable Futures, 4, 100077. 
Why it matters 
Drawing on a review of 128 scholarly sources and empirical data from 30 technology startups, 
this study develops a framework for understanding the challenges that emerging technology 
ventures encounter during their earliest stages. 
The barriers extend beyond financing and include organizational capacity, market readiness, 
technology maturity, partnerships, and commercialization strategy. 
Consider: 
Are today's innovation programs addressing the entire commercialization journey—or primarily 
the funding gap? 
DOI: 10.1016/j.sftr.2022.100077 
9. Policy Barriers to Translation

Meslin, E. M., Blasimme, A., & Cambon-Thomsen, A. (2013). Mapping the translational 
science policy “valley of death.” Clinical and Translational Medicine, 2, 14. 
Why it matters 
This article broadens the valley-of-death concept by demonstrating that policy itself can create 
barriers to translation. Regulatory, ethical, institutional, and governance environments can slow 
progress just as significantly as scientific or financial limitations. 
Consider: 
What policies or administrative practices intended to support or protect innovation may 
unintentionally slow it? 
DOI: 10.1186/2001-1326-2-14 
Universities, Technology Transfer, and 
Entrepreneurship 
10. Technology Transfer Offices and Commercialization 
Siegel, D. S., Veugelers, R., & Wright, M. (2007). Technology transfer offices and 
commercialization of university intellectual property: Performance and policy 
implications. Oxford Review of Economic Policy, 23(4), 640–660. 
Why it matters 
The authors examine university technology-transfer performance and conclude that universities 
and regions need coherent, feasible commercialization strategies rather than treating patenting 
and licensing as isolated activities. 
Consider: 
Should the next generation of technology-transfer organizations be measured primarily by 
patents and licenses—or by broader measures of economic and societal impact? 
DOI: 10.1093/oxrep/grm036 
11. Academic Entrepreneurship After Bayh-Dole 
Grimaldi, R., Kenney, M., Siegel, D. S., & Wright, M. (2011). 30 years after Bayh-Dole: 
Reassessing academic entrepreneurship. Research Policy, 40(8), 1045–1057. 
Why it matters

This article examines the evolution of university commercialization and academic 
entrepreneurship since enactment of the Bayh-Dole Act and considers the institutional, 
managerial, and policy structures influencing university-based innovation. 
Consider: 
Are university incentives, promotion systems, intellectual-property practices, and organizational 
structures adequately designed to support translation and entrepreneurship? 
DOI: 10.1016/j.respol.2011.04.005 
Innovation Ecosystems 
12. Measuring Entrepreneurial Ecosystems 
Johnson, E. E., Hemmatian, I., Lanahan, L., & Joshi, A. M. (2022). A framework and 
databases for measuring entrepreneurial ecosystems. Research Policy, 51(2), 104398. 
Why it matters 
This work places relationships among entrepreneurs, firms, government organizations, 
accelerators, and research institutions at the center of entrepreneurial ecosystem performance. It 
also highlights multiple roles government can play, including catalyst, coordinator, certifier, and 
customer. 
Consider: 
What indicators should the United States use to determine whether an innovation ecosystem is 
truly healthy and productive? 
DOI: 10.1016/j.respol.2021.104398

PART II 
NATIONAL INNOV ATION POLICY & 
STRATEGY RESOURCES 
1. NSF FY 2026–2030 Strategic Plan 
The current NSF Strategic Plan identifies transformative research and innovation, U.S. 
technological leadership, STEM talent, partnerships, and measurable impact as major agency 
priorities. It also asks an especially relevant question for the NIC: how NSF can better measure 
the outcomes and impact of its investments in U.S. research and innovation. 
NIC relevance: 
This provides an important overarching framework for considering how federal research 
investments translate into economic growth, national security, technological leadership, and 
societal benefit. 
Roadmap question: 
How should the United States define and measure the impact of publicly supported 
innovation? 
2. NSF Directorate for Technology, Innovation and 
Partnerships — TIP 
NSF TIP focuses explicitly on use-inspired and translational research and organizes its work 
around accelerating critical and emerging technologies, expanding the geography of innovation, 
and building a competition-ready workforce. Its portfolio includes Accelerating Research 
Translation, I-Corps, Regional Innovation Engines, Translation to Practice, America's Seed 
Fund, technology accelerators, and other programs. 
NIC relevance: 
TIP provides a contemporary federal model for connecting research, technology translation, 
entrepreneurship, regional development, workforce, and industry partnerships. 
Roadmap question: 
What gaps remain between excellent federal innovation programs and the ability to move 
discoveries rapidly and routinely into use? 
3. NSF Innovation Corps — I-Corps™ 

PAGE 9
9 | P a g e  
 
NSF I-Corps provides immersive entrepreneurial education that helps scientists and engineers 
move beyond the laboratory, understand customer and market needs, and accelerate the 
transformation of invention into impact. NSF identifies technology translation, entrepreneurial 
workforce development, economic impact, and innovation ecosystems among the program's 
central objectives. 
NIC relevance: 
I-Corps demonstrates the importance of combining technology development with customer 
discovery, mentorship, market understanding, and entrepreneurial capability. 
Roadmap question: 
Which elements of the I-Corps model should be expanded, adapted, or connected more 
directly with later stages of commercialization? 
4. NSF Translation to Practice 
NSF's Translation to Practice initiative supports use-inspired research, translational activity, 
partnerships, commercial applications, market learning, and industry adoption. NSF explicitly 
recognizes that translation from scientific discovery to societal impact often follows nonlinear 
pathways. 
NIC relevance: 
The program directly addresses one of the central questions of the Convening: how the national 
innovation system can shorten the distance between research and real-world application. 
Roadmap question: 
What support is most commonly missing between proof-of-concept and adoption? 
5. NSF Regional Innovation Engines 
NSF Engines are designed to create sustainable, place-based innovation ecosystems built around 
cross-sector coalitions involving academia, industry, government, nonprofits, communities, 
entrepreneurs, and workforce organizations. Their core functions include use-inspired R&D, 
translation of innovation results to society, and workforce development. 
NIC relevance: 
The Engines model encourages participants to consider innovation as an ecosystem-level 
challenge, rather than simply the responsibility of individual researchers or startups. 
Roadmap question: 
What makes an innovation ecosystem self-sustaining after initial federal investment ends?

6. America's AI Action Plan 
The federal AI strategy emphasizes acceleration of AI innovation, development of supporting 
infrastructure, workforce and scientific capacity, and strengthening U.S. leadership in artificial 
intelligence. 
NIC relevance: 
AI should be considered not only as an industry or emerging technology, but also as a potential 
horizontal innovation accelerator capable of affecting discovery, design, modeling, 
experimentation, decision-making, manufacturing, commercialization, and workforce 
development. 
Roadmap question: 
Where can AI responsibly remove time, cost, and friction from today's innovation process? 
7. National Academies — Strategic Innovation and 
Commercialization 
National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. Strategic Innovation and 
Commercialization: Supporting IP and Tech Transfer to Advance U.S. Research 
Competitiveness. 
This National Academies resource examines intellectual property, technology transfer, 
commercialization, partnerships, and research competitiveness. 
NIC relevance: 
It invites consideration of whether today's technology-transfer structures adequately support an 
innovation system increasingly characterized by interdisciplinary research, rapid technological 
change, global competition, and cross-sector partnerships. 
Roadmap question: 
Which parts of the current technology-transfer system enable commercialization, and 
which create unnecessary friction? 
8. NIST — Federal Technology Transfer and Laboratory-to-
Market Activities 
NIST plays an important coordinating role in federal technology transfer and tracks mechanisms 
including patents, licensing, Cooperative Research and Development Agreements, and other 
approaches for transferring federally developed technologies toward commercial or societal use. 
NIC relevance: 
Federal laboratories represent an enormous source of research expertise and intellectual property,

yet their integration with universities, startups, investors, and regional ecosystems can vary 
considerably. 
Roadmap question: 
How can federal laboratories participate more seamlessly in America's broader innovation 
ecosystem? 
9. CHIPS for America — Research and Development 
Programs 
CHIPS R&D provides an instructive model of a mission-driven innovation strategy intended to 
connect invention, development, prototyping, deployment, workforce development, and 
commercial scale. NIST describes three goals: building domestic capacity to invent and deploy 
semiconductor technologies, accelerating ideas to market, and developing the necessary 
semiconductor workforce. 
NIC relevance: 
The CHIPS model provides an opportunity to examine whether integrated approaches involving 
shared infrastructure, prototyping, workforce investment, industry participation, and 
commercialization could be applied to other critical technology areas. 
Roadmap question: 
What aspects of mission-driven models such as CHIPS could accelerate commercialization 
in other industries?

PART III 
PRE-CONVENING REFLECTION 
QUESTIONS 
Participants are encouraged to reflect on the following questions before arriving at the National 
Innovation Convening. 
1. Identify the Barrier 
What is the single greatest barrier you encounter—or observe—in moving promising 
discoveries toward implementation or commercialization? 
Consider barriers involving: 
• Funding 
• Technology readiness 
• Market validation 
• Regulatory processes 
• Intellectual property 
• University policies 
• Technology-transfer processes 
• Access to industry partners 
• Access to investors 
• Entrepreneurial expertise 
• Talent and workforce 
• Infrastructure and prototyping 
• Risk tolerance 
• Organizational culture 
• Federal policies or administrative requirements 
2. Identify the Intervention 
If you could change one policy, practice, incentive, program, or institutional structure to 
remove that barrier, what would you change? 
Participants should be prepared to move beyond describing problems and suggest actionable 
interventions. 
3. Consider the Innovation Journey 
Think about the pathway:

DISCOVERY → TRANSLATION → VALIDATION → 
COMMERCIALIZATION → SCALE → IMPACT 
Where does the innovation process most frequently slow down or fail? 
What resources, partnerships, capabilities, or policies are missing at that stage? 
4. Consider Artificial Intelligence 
Where could AI have the greatest positive impact on the innovation lifecycle? 
Consider: 
• Scientific discovery 
• Literature and knowledge synthesis 
• Experimental design 
• Engineering design 
• Simulation and digital twins 
• Prototyping 
• Customer discovery 
• Market analysis 
• Intellectual-property analysis 
• Regulatory preparation 
• Manufacturing 
• Supply-chain planning 
• Entrepreneurship 
• Investor readiness 
• Technology-transfer processes 
Also consider: 
Where should AI not replace human judgment? 
5. Consider the Innovation Ecosystem 
Which relationships must become stronger for innovation to move faster? 
Examples may include: 
University ↔ Industry 
University ↔ Investors 
Researchers ↔ Entrepreneurs 
Federal Agencies ↔ Industry 
Federal Laboratories ↔ Universities 
Startups ↔ Large Corporations 

PAGE 14
14 | P a g e  
 
Research Institutions ↔ Communities 
Universities ↔ Regional Economic Development Organizations 
What currently prevents those relationships from working effectively? 
6. Consider Incentives 
Are researchers, universities, companies, federal laboratories, investors, and other 
innovation stakeholders rewarded for the behaviors necessary to accelerate innovation? 
If not: 
What incentives need to change? 
7. Consider Access to Innovation 
Who currently has access to America's innovation ecosystem—and who does not? 
Consider geographic location, institutional size, research infrastructure, access to capital, access 
to networks, workforce availability, and participation by emerging innovation regions. 
How can the nation's innovation capacity be expanded beyond the institutions and regions 
that have historically dominated technology commercialization? 
8. Consider Scale 
The United States has many successful innovation programs. 
The larger question is whether successful models can be connected and scaled. 
Which existing program, model, or practice should be expanded nationally? 
What would be required to do so?

THE NATIONAL INNOV ATION 
ROADMAP CHALLENGE 
Throughout the Convening, participants will be asked to move from: 
Observation → Barrier Identification → Root Cause → Intervention → Action 
The goal is not simply to identify what is wrong with the innovation system. 
The goal is to identify what can be changed. 
As you prepare for the National Innovation Convening, consider: 
If the United States could make only three major changes during the next five 
years to dramatically accelerate innovation discovery and commercialization, 
what should those changes be?
