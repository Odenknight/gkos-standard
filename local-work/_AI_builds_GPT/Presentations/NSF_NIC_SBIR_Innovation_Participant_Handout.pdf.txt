PRE-CONVENING READING HANDOUT 
Purpose. These four readings offer complementary—and at times competing—perspectives on how federal small-
business innovation programs can better move promising technologies from research and early validation to adoption, 
procurement, and commercial impact. Together, they raise questions central to the National Innovation Convening: 
how to reward outcomes, preserve experimentation, broaden participation, reduce friction, and strengthen the 
pathways that connect discovery to use. 
As you read, consider: What should the federal innovation system optimize for—commercial sales, mission impact, 
technical learning, new-firm participation, follow-on investment, procurement transition, or some combination? Where 
are the most consequential bottlenecks, and which actors are best positioned to remove them?  
The readings are summarized below to support discussion; participants are encouraged to consult the full articles for the authors’ 
complete arguments and evidence. 
1. Nurturing America’s Seed Fund 
Gail Cohen, Maryann P. Feldman, and Scott Stern | Issues in Science and Technology | Summer 2026 
Cohen, Feldman, and Stern argue that SBIR/STTR should be understood as a mission -oriented innovation 
infrastructure rather than as a single-purpose commercialization program. Drawing on National Academies 
evaluations across DOE, NIH, NSF, and DOD, they conclude that the p rograms generate meaningful innovation and 
transition outcomes, but that success looks different across agency missions. The authors caution against uniform rules 
or a single performance metric and instead call for long-term program stability, stronger technology-transition 
pathways, reduced administrative bottlenecks, broader participation, and much better integrated data.  
Key ideas for participants 
• Agency context matters: commercialization can mean FDA progress at NIH, private-capital attraction at NSF, energy-
technology deployment at DOE, or integration into defense systems at DOD. 
• Multiple-award firms should not automatically be treated as program failures; in some mission environments, repeated 
awards build specialized capabilities and institutional knowledge. 
• The authors favor multifaceted evaluation that captures technical progress, mission value, follow-on funding, procurement, 
private investment, and other forms of transition. 
• Priority reforms include permanence or greater funding stability, better Phase III/transition mechanisms, faster processes, 
inclusive outreach, and stronger data infrastructure. 
NIC CONNECTION  A national innovation roadmap may need to preserve mission -specific flexibility while creating 
shared measures of progress, stronger transition infrastructure, and better cross-agency learning. 
2. Reauthorizing the Small Business Innovation Research Program Isn’t Enough 
Lars Erik Schönander | Issues in Science and Technology | Spring 2026 
Schönander argues that reauthorization is necessary but insufficient because SBIR’s larger challenge is the 
commercialization ecosystem surrounding the program. He highlights two concerns: long -term dependence by some 
“multiple award winners” that do not demonstrate sufficient commercialization, and the difficulty many technically 
promising firms face in scaling from feasibility to market or procurement. His proposed reforms seek both to open the

program to new entrants and to strengthen the path across the  “valley of death,” including experimental selection 
mechanisms, commercialization-readiness assessment, matched funding, gap funding, and stronger university

Key ideas for participants 
• Reform should address both incumbent dependence and barriers that discourage capable first-time applicants. 
• Possible experiments include qualified-proposal lotteries and occasional “golden tickets” for high-risk, high-reward ideas 
that conventional consensus review may miss. 
• Commercialization readiness should be assessed explicitly through market, regulatory, supply-chain, customer, and techno-
economic evidence—not technical merit alone. 
• Universities are critical to the pipeline; promotion incentives, complex licensing practices, and weak translation support can 
impede academic entrepreneurship. 
NIC CONNECTION  Innovation acceleration requires attention beyond the grant itself: selection, customer 
discovery, adoption readiness, gap funding, university technology transfer, procurement, and scale-up must operate 
as a connected system. 
3. Ideas for Improving the SBIR Program 
Forum response by Warren Katz, Chair, The Alliance for Commercial Technology in Government | Issues in Science and Technology | 
Summer 2026 
This response takes a more prescriptive view of SBIR reform. Katz argues that the program should more clearly 
function as seed funding that helps firms commercialize and then graduate, rather than becoming a recurring source of 
operating revenue. He contends that persistent “SBIR mills ” can crowd out newcomers and lock up intellectual 
property without sufficient market impact. To sharpen incentives toward commercialization, he recommends a lifetime 
funding cap, a smaller employee-size threshold, much heavier weighting of commercial viab ility in award decisions, 
and dedicated DOD procurement resources to help promising technologies reach end users.  
Key ideas for participants 
• The central performance test should be whether public R&D support leads toward products, services, customers, and  non-
SBIR revenue. 
• Katz proposes restoring a $75 million lifetime SBIR funding cap per company and reducing the employee eligibility 
threshold from 500 to 200. 
• He recommends making commercial viability 50% of award criteria, evaluated by professionals with relevant 
commercialization expertise. 
• He also calls for a standing DOD procurement-side budget to support end-user matches for the new Strategic Breakthrough 
production phase. 
NIC CONNECTION  This perspective pushes participants to ask when continued public R&D support is productive 
persistence and when it becomes dependency—and how the system can create clearer graduation and procurement 
pathways. 
4. SBA Looks to Right-Size Small Business Rules…Again 
Terry Gerton interview with Emily Murphy | Federal News Network | August 25, 2026 
This interview examines the Small Business Administration’s proposed changes to federal small -business size 
standards and connects that debate to SBIR policy. Murphy describes the tension between simplifying eligibili ty rules, 
allowing firms room to grow, and protecting genuinely small firms from competition with much larger companies. On 
SBIR, she argues that the more useful question is not simply how many awards a company has received, but what those 
awards produced. She advocates stronger past-performance information that captures whether Phase I and II work 
advanced R&D, generated useful learning, and improved the prospects for transition and commercialization.

Key ideas for participants 
• Eligibility design can unintentionally create either barriers to growth or competitive disadvantages for smaller firms.  
• Simplifying size standards may reduce administrative complexity, but broad categories can group firms with very different 
economic characteristics. 
• Repeated SBIR awards should be evaluated through results and progress rather than award counts alone. 
• Better cross-agency sharing of SBIR past-performance and technical-learning information could improve future award 
decisions and Phase III commercialization. 
NIC CONNECTION  The interview reinforces a recurring NIC theme: policy design should reward demonstrable 
progress and learning while avoiding one-size-fits-all rules that distort competition or discourage growth. 
Cross-Cutting Themes for NIC Discussion 
Define success broadly—but clearly. The readings disagree on how strongly commercialization should dominate 
evaluation, but all emphasize the need to connect public R&D investments to meaningful outcomes.  
Fix the transition system, not only the award system. Customer discovery, procurement, gap funding, regulatory 
pathways, private capital, licensing, and scale-up repeatedly emerge as decisive constraints. 
Balance new entrants with experienced performers. Broadening participation is important, but experience can also 
create mission-relevant capability. Better performance data can help distinguish productive persistence from 
dependency. 
Reduce friction while preserving rigor. Long proposals, slow reviews, complex contracting, size rules, and university 
licensing can deter innovators. Simplification should target real bottlenecks without lowering technical or mission 
standards. 
Build a learning innovation system. Integrated data and cross-agency visibility can help policymakers and program 
managers understand what works, for whom, in which mission context, and why.
